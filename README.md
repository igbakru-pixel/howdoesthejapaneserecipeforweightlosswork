<h2><strong>Washoku: how traditional Japanese meals are built</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Washoku is the traditional Japanese way of eating.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Healthline describes it as small dishes of simple, fresh and seasonal ingredients, and says it is rich in vegetables, uses small portion sizes and is naturally low in added sugar and fat.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Those factors, it says, contribute to a lower calorie count.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Read the overview of the</span><a href="https://www.healthline.com/nutrition/japanese-diet-scored"> <span style="font-weight: 400;">Japanese diet</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> for more detail.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">A typical meal follows a simple structure: a bowl of rice, a main dish such as grilled fish or tofu, a soup, a vegetable side and a small pickle.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">This idea of staple, main and side dishes also appears in Japan's national food guidelines.</span></p>
<h2><strong>Hara hachi bu: stopping at about 80% full</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Hara hachi bu is a Japanese practice of eating until you are about 80% full.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The evidence is still limited.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">A ScienceDaily report notes that research on it is limited, and that earlier studies looked at overall eating patterns in regions where the practice is common, not the 80% rule on its own. It also says the approach is gaining attention as a strategy for weight loss and may reduce emotional eating.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">See the summary on hara hachi bu.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">A practical way to try it: serve smaller portions, eat slowly, put your chopsticks or fork down between bites, and stop when you are no longer hungry, not when the plate is empty.</span></p>

<h2><strong>A simple Japanese-inspired meal to make at home</strong></h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Here is a gentle, balanced dinner you can try. It makes two servings.</span></p>
<p><strong>Miso soup with tofu and wakame</strong></p>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">2 cups dashi (or water with a splash of dashi stock)</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">1 to 2 tablespoons miso paste</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">100 g soft tofu, cubed</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">1 tablespoon dried wakame</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">1 sliced green onion</span></li>
</ul>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Heat the dashi until it steams, but don't boil it.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Add the tofu and wakame, and cook for two minutes.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Turn off the heat, stir the miso in until it dissolves, and top with green onion.&nbsp;</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Miso can be salty, so people who limit sodium should ask their doctor.</span></p>
<p><strong>Simple plate</strong></p>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">A small bowl of cooked rice</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">A palm-sized portion of grilled or baked fish, or extra tofu</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">A side of steamed or lightly saut&eacute;ed vegetables, such as spinach, mushrooms or green beans</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">A few pickled cucumbers or radishes</span></li>
</ul>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Eat slowly, drink water or green tea, and stop when you feel about 80% full.</span></p>
